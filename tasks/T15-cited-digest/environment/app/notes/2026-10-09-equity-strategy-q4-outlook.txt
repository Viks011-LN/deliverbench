Title: Equity strategy: the fourth quarter
Date: 2026-10-09
Desk: Strategy

We enter the last quarter of the year neutral on equities, with a preference for companies that can
grow earnings without help from the economy.

The backdrop

Cash still pays, which raises the bar for every other asset. We stay neutral on equities against
bonds, with a preference for quality and cash flow. We review the allocation at the start of each
quarter and after any large market move. Our view of inflation-linked bonds is constructive: the
case is sound, but much of it is in the price. We prefer to express a view on growth through credit
rather than through cyclical equities. Earnings, not multiples, will have to do the work next year.
Volatility has been low for most of the quarter, and hedges are cheap by historical standards.

Survey evidence points to a tentative recovery in services, while manufacturing remains the weak
spot. Services inflation is proving stickier than goods inflation, which has all but stopped rising.
The labour market is loosening at the margin, with vacancies falling faster than employment.
Business surveys have become a less reliable guide to output, so we put more weight on hard data.
The housing market is responding to lower mortgage rates more slowly than in past cycles. Housing
activity has bottomed, but a strong rebound is unlikely while mortgage rates stay where they are.
Household spending has held up better than confidence surveys suggested, helped by real wage gains.
We see the risks to activity as balanced in the near term and tilted to the downside next year.

Earnings

Capital spending plans are rising for the first time in two years. Analysts' estimates for next year
still assume a recovery in volumes that the companies do not promise. Guidance has been conservative
this season, which leaves room for upgrades in the new year. Sentiment toward food retail held up
over the quarter so far as insurers digested the supply of new bonds. We would use any weakness
after results to add to our preferred names. Valuations in the sector sit close to their ten-year
average, with the discount to the market narrowing. Buybacks are an increasing share of shareholder
returns across the sector. Dividend cover is comfortable for most of the companies we follow.

Sectors

Supply chains have normalised, and component shortages are no longer holding back deliveries.
Engineering capacity, not demand, is now the main constraint on growth. Rail and grid equipment
remain the strongest end markets, with orders running ahead of deliveries. Working capital has risen
with the order book, as customers pay later in the project cycle. Large framework orders from
national operators give the sector unusual visibility into next year.

Private-label share gains have slowed as branded producers cut promotional prices. The best
performers have combined price increases with investment in their brands. Volumes remain the weak
spot, with consumers trading down within categories rather than out of them. Marketing spending is
recovering after two years of cuts. Input-cost hedges roll off over the winter, so next year's cost
base is easier to see than usual.

Refining exposure has helped the integrated companies this year. Windfall taxes remain a political
risk, though none is on the table now. Decommissioning costs are a growing item in the cash-flow
statements of older producers. Low-carbon spending is rising, but from a small base and at lower
returns than the core business. Hedging policies vary widely across the sector, which makes results
harder to compare.

Where we stand on the names we cover

Norrland Industrial remains our top pick in capital goods: its order book gives it the clearest view
of next year's revenue. Corvo Telecom is a defensive holding whose results should show the benefit
of price increases. Bellamy Foods and Pellmark Retail sit on opposite sides of the same argument
about pricing, and we hold both. Sorvik Energy is our way to own the gas price.

Management teams have become more cautious about the second half in their outlook statements.
Share-price reactions to results have rewarded cash flow more than revenue growth. Management
changes have been unusually frequent across the sector this year. Working capital has been a drag on
cash flow this year as companies rebuilt stocks. Pricing power is the theme of the season: companies
that held prices have protected their margins. We prefer companies with net cash and pricing power
over those that rely on volume growth. The sector trades at a premium to its history because of the
visibility of its earnings.

Rates and the multiple

Curve steepening has been the theme of the fortnight as term premia rose across the region. Central
bank communication has shifted from guidance to data dependence, which adds to volatility. The term
structure of volatility slopes upward: the market fears the new year more than the old. Volatility
in the front end has fallen to its lowest since the start of the cycle. We would fade any move that
relies on one data release alone. Sentiment toward equities was little changed over the month as
pension funds digested the pace of wage growth.

Political risk is low on our list, but it is rarely priced until it matters. The year end has a
habit of exaggerating moves in thin markets, so we keep position sizes small. Dispersion within
sectors remains high, which favours stock selection over sector bets. Our allocation is built to
survive a range of outcomes rather than to win on one. The correlation between bonds and equities
has turned negative again, which restores the value of balance.

Positioning

We keep a small overweight in credit, funded from government bonds. The backdrop is one of slowing
but positive growth and inflation drifting lower. Asset managers have added to covered bonds, and
positioning there is no longer light. Consensus positioning is long quality and short cyclicals,
which leaves room for a squeeze.

The equity volatility index ended the month at 13.5. Equity funds lost USD 8.5bn over the fortnight,
and bond funds took in USD 4.1bn. Two-year swap rates in the major markets moved by 5bp on average
over the month.

Hybrid issuance has picked up as issuers lock in funding before rates fall further. We prefer
industrial and utility paper over financials at current levels. Rating drift has turned slightly
positive, with upgrades outnumbering downgrades this quarter. Primary markets were busy, with
issuers bringing deals forward ahead of results blackouts. Retail investors have been net buyers of
defensive equities for most of the fortnight.

Portfolio flows matter more for the currency than the trade figures, and they have been narrow.
Options markets price a calm year end, which we think is too complacent. Asset managers have trimmed
their long dollar positions, but only at the margin. We see little reason to chase the latest move
and prefer to sell volatility.

What would change our mind

Three things would make us more positive: a clear turn in manufacturing orders, a fall in long-term
yields that does not come with weaker growth, and evidence that consumers are spending their
real-income gains rather than saving them. Two would make us more cautious: a rise in unemployment
that firms did not plan, and a renewed rise in energy prices over the winter.

Auction demand has been uneven, with cover ratios close to their averages for the year. We prefer to
hold steepeners in the five-to-thirty-year sector. Supply is the main near-term risk for longer
maturities, with heavy issuance scheduled before the year end. Swap spreads have tightened as demand
from pension funds picked up into the quarter end.

Cost programmes announced last year are now showing up in the margins. Input-cost relief from lower
freight and energy is now flowing through to gross margins. Earnings revisions have turned less
negative, and the season so far has delivered more beats than misses. Labour costs remain the
biggest unknown for next year's margins.

Risks

Risks to our view include a sharper fall in energy prices, a faster easing of the labour market and
a renewed rise in long-term yields. Any of these would change the balance of our arguments.

The central banks of the region are close to the end of their cycles, but not all at the same point.
Public investment is set to rise next year as infrastructure programmes move from planning to
building. Investment intentions are strongest in energy and logistics, and weakest in construction.
Inventories were run down over the summer, and the restocking cycle has further to go. Government
bond issuance plans for next year will be published with the budget, and we expect few surprises.

Forward curves have flattened, signalling a better-supplied market next year. Weather is the main
swing factor for gas over the winter, and the long-range forecasts are no help. Long-dated prices
have moved far less than prompt prices this year. We keep our price assumptions unchanged and see
the risks as balanced. Stocks held by consumers of industrial metals are low, which supports prices
on any recovery.

This note reflects the desk's views at the time of writing. Published figures are from the sources
