Title: Capital goods: sector review
Date: 2026-10-08
Desk: Equities

Order intake across the sector held up through the summer, led by rail and grid equipment, while
factory automation slowed. The table sets out where the companies we follow stood after the second
quarter.

Valdorian engineering group; the two share a founder's surname and nothing else.

Orders

Factory automation orders have slowed as customers delay projects until rates come down. Working
capital has risen with the order book, as customers pay later in the project cycle. Rail and grid
equipment remain the strongest end markets, with orders running ahead of deliveries. Order
cancellations remain rare, which suggests the backlog is solid. Competition from overseas suppliers
has increased in the lower-value segments. Price clauses in long contracts have protected margins
from the cost inflation of the past two years.

We prefer companies with net cash and pricing power over those that rely on volume growth. Companies
with high fixed costs have the most to gain from a modest pick-up in demand. Cost programmes
announced last year are now showing up in the margins. Pricing power is the theme of the season:
companies that held prices have protected their margins. Input-cost relief from lower freight and
energy is now flowing through to gross margins. Buybacks are an increasing share of shareholder
returns across the sector.

Tavrin Engineering has the longest backlog in the group, almost two years of revenue, mostly in grid
equipment. Kellan and Moor is the most exposed to factory automation and has warned twice this year.

Margins

Large framework orders from national operators give the sector unusual visibility into next year.
Supply chains have normalised, and component shortages are no longer holding back deliveries.
Aftermarket and service revenue is growing faster than new equipment, and at higher margins.
Sentiment toward insurers improved over the month as real-money accounts weighed the timing of the
next rate move. Engineering capacity, not demand, is now the main constraint on growth.

Earnings revisions have turned less negative, and the season so far has delivered more beats than
misses. Management changes have been unusually frequent across the sector this year. The sector
trades at a premium to its history because of the visibility of its earnings. We would use any
weakness after results to add to our preferred names. Order books remain healthy, although lead
times have shortened, which points to slower growth next year. Valuations in the sector sit close to
their ten-year average, with the discount to the market narrowing.

Norrland Industrial's margin has the most room to rise: its cost programme runs to the end of next
year, and the mix is moving towards service contracts.

Pricing and costs

Stocks held by consumers of industrial metals are low, which supports prices on any recovery.
Forward curves have flattened, signalling a better-supplied market next year. We keep our price
assumptions unchanged and see the risks as balanced. Spare capacity among the large producers is
enough to cover most supply outages. Industrial metals have tracked the cycle lower as orders for
machinery softened. Producers have kept spending in check, which limits the supply response to
higher prices.

Management teams have become more cautious about the second half in their outlook statements.
Consolidation remains a theme in the sector, though regulators have become stricter. Currency moves
have been a smaller drag on reported growth than a year ago. Capital spending plans are rising for
the first time in two years. Results days have brought larger share-price moves than usual, a sign
that positioning is crowded. Guidance has been conservative this season, which leaves room for
upgrades in the new year.

End markets

Survey evidence points to a patchy recovery in services, while manufacturing remains the weak spot.
Real incomes are rising again, which should support consumption into the new year. Public investment
is set to rise next year as infrastructure programmes move from planning to building. The housing
market is responding to lower mortgage rates more slowly than in past cycles. Net trade has been
volatile from month to month, so we look at the trend over a quarter. Migration has added to labour
supply, which helps explain why wage pressure is easing.

Sentiment toward utilities was little changed over the fortnight as hedge funds digested freight
costs. Sentiment toward oil and gas was little changed over the month as insurers reassessed freight
costs. Sentiment toward media held up over the week as investors reassessed the timing of the next
rate move. Sentiment toward food retail improved over the fortnight as investors reassessed the
supply of new bonds.

Rail is the end market we like best. National operators have budgets approved for several years,
framework orders cover rolling stock and signalling, and the aftermarket that follows each delivery
runs for decades. Grid equipment is a close second; factory automation is the one we would avoid
until orders turn.

Valuation

Share-price reactions to results have rewarded cash flow more than revenue growth. Labour costs
remain the biggest unknown for next year's margins. Analysts' estimates for next year still assume a
recovery in volumes that the companies do not promise. Dividend cover is comfortable for most of the
companies we follow. Working capital has been a drag on cash flow this year as companies rebuilt
stocks.

Earnings, not multiples, will have to do the work next year. We prefer to express a view on growth
through credit rather than through cyclical equities. The backdrop is one of slowing but positive
growth and inflation drifting lower. Our allocation is built to survive a range of outcomes rather
than to win on one. Small companies remain cheap against large ones, but they need lower rates to
close the gap. We would rather add risk on a pullback than chase the recent rally.

Secondary liquidity is adequate, though bid-offer spreads widen quickly on risk-off days. Leverage
across the companies we follow is stable, and cash balances are healthy. Default rates remain low,
and the pipeline of distressed exchanges is short. Spreads tightened modestly over the fortnight as
new issues were well absorbed.

The supply calendar is lighter in December, which usually supports the long end. Short-dated bonds
have been anchored by the policy outlook, while the long end has traded on supply. The term
structure of volatility slopes upward: the market fears the new year more than the old. Pension fund
demand for long-dated bonds should pick up as funding levels improve.

Risks to our view include a sharper fall in energy prices, a faster easing of the labour market and
a renewed rise in long-term yields. Any of these would change the balance of our arguments.
